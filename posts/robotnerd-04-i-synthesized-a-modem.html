0x4: I Synthesized a Modem

DATE: 2026-07-15 | PROJECT: ROBOTNERD

Every app needs a soul. Most find it in the interaction design, the typography, the motion. RobotNerd found it in a 300-baud acoustic coupler.

This is the devlog I most wanted to write, because the audio engine is the heart of this project, and it's the part that's hardest to explain without sounding unhinged. So let me just say what it is and then show you how.

The app doesn't have any audio files. Every sound it makes is synthesized in real time, sample by sample, at 44.1 kHz, in about 600 lines of Kotlin.

There's a drone bed of ninth chords played as dub-techno stabs through a dotted-eighth feedback delay and a Schroeder reverb. There's a noise texture of pink noise through resonant filters. There are bell accents. And — the bit that makes people look up — the agent's actual response text is modulated onto Bell 103 modem carriers and you hear it chirp.

The agent talks in modem. Let me explain.

Why Not Just Play a Sound?

The journey started normal. Send a message → boop. Get a response → bloop. Generating → ticking. Three one-shot samples, triggered on events. Fine. Boring. And, as it turned out, annoying — sharp transient sounds fired repeatedly get grating fast. The first feedback was "some of these are super sharp and it sounds like it's about to hit."

So I had a choice: curate better samples, or don't use samples at all.

The second option is harder but more interesting. If you synthesize the sound, you control its entire character — the timbre, the envelope, the evolution. You can make it breathe. A notification is a moment; a soundscape is a presence. I wanted the app to feel present. So I threw out the samples and started building an engine.

Layer 1: FSK Modem Synthesis

This is the heart. FSK — Frequency-Shift Keying — is how old modems worked: a 1 bit is one frequency, a 0 bit is another, and you switch between them at a baud rate. The classic Bell 103 standard (300 baud, the original acoustic-coupler modem) uses:

  • Originate channel: mark = 1270 Hz, space = 1070 Hz
  • Answer channel: mark = 2225 Hz, space = 2025 Hz

These are real numbers from a real 1970s standard. And here's the magic trick: I feed the agent's actual response bytes into the FSK encoder.

fun feedData(text: String) {
    for (b in text.toByteArray(Charsets.UTF_8)) {
        for (bit in 7 downTo 0) {
            bitQueue.offer(((b.toInt() shr bit) and 1) == 1)
        }
    }
}

When the agent responds, its words are converted to bytes, each byte to bits, and each bit selects a carrier frequency. The result: you hear the agent's response as a warbling, chirping modem tone. You can't decode it by ear (I mean, you almost can, if you're a 1983 modem), but it sounds exactly right — the data-transfer chatter of a connection being made. The carrier alternates between originate and answer channels every few seconds, so it has that authentic handshake character.

It's a gimmick in the best sense: technically genuine, sonically evocative, and deeply on-brand for a thing called RobotNerd.

Layer 2: The Dub Techno Drone

The FSK alone would be thin and buzzy. The bed underneath it is a dub techno engine — think Basic Channel, Maurizio, Deepchord. The signature sound of that genre: staccato chord stabs fed into a long feedback delay, where the echoes are the actual sustained sound and the dry stabs are just seeds.

Here's how it's built, layer by layer:

The chords are ninth chords — Am9, Dm9, Cmaj9, Fmaj9, and friends. Five voices each (root, third, fifth, seventh, ninth). An eight-chord progression that cycles, each chord held 16–52 seconds. Ninth chords because they're lush and ambiguous — never quite resolving, always drifting.

The oscillator is a sine/sawtooth blend. Pure sine is too clean; pure saw is too harsh. A Fourier-style saw (sum of harmonics) mixed with a sine gives warmth without screech.

The rhythm is staccato gating at ~120 BPM. Short rhythmic pulses — 5ms attack, 90ms sustain, 50ms release, then rest — with patterns that evolve every 4–8 bars (eighth-note stabs, quarter-note, syncopated, sparse). This is what makes it groove instead of just drone.

The delay is the secret weapon: a feedback delay tuned to a dotted eighth (0.75× the beat). That specific delay time is the entire dub techno aesthetic — the echoes fall between the beats, creating a rolling, off-grid momentum. And the feedback path has a one-pole low-pass, so each echo is progressively darker than the last. That darkening is the classic "dub echo" sound — the repeats get muddier and warmer as they decay.

The reverb is a hand-built Schroeder reverb: four comb filters in parallel (the classic Freeverb delay lengths — 1116, 1188, 1277, 1356 samples) feeding two all-pass filters in series for diffusion. It's a cavernous, dense tail. The dry stabs are quiet; the delay + reverb cloud is the sound.

The master mix balances it: a little dry stab, a lot of delay, a wash of reverb.

The Breakthrough: Harmonic Locking

Here's the problem I almost shipped, and the fix that saved it.

The FSK used fixed Bell 103 frequencies: 1270/1070 and 2225/2025 Hz. But the drone underneath is playing chords — Am9, Dm9, etc. And 1270 Hz has no harmonic relationship to any of those notes. So the modem chirps clashed with the chord bed. Dissonant. Harsh. "It sounds like it's about to hit."

The fix was elegant: derive the FSK carrier frequencies from the current chord. Instead of fixed 1270/1070, use the chord's root and third. Instead of 2225/2025, use the fifth and ninth, raised two octaves. Now the modem carriers are always harmonically locked to whatever chord is playing — they're intervals within the harmony, not random fixed pitches fighting it.

if (usingAnswerChannel) {
    markFreq = droneVoices[2].freq * 4f   // 5th, up 2 octaves
    spaceFreq = droneVoices[4].freq * 4f  // 9th, up 2 octaves
} else {
    markFreq = droneVoices[0].freq * octaveMul  // root
    spaceFreq = droneVoices[1].freq * octaveMul // third
}

The modem stopped clashing and started singing with the drone. Same data chatter, now in tune. That single change transformed the character from "annoying" to "alive." It's the detail that makes the whole engine cohere.

Layer 3: The Training Sequence

Because a modem isn't just data — it connects first. Real modems emit a warbling, stuttering training sequence before the handshake: the two ends negotiate, equalize, sync up. It's that unmistakable bzzz-bzzz-bzzz you heard in every 90s movie phone scene.

So there's a training layer too — but with a twist: it cycles through four different carrier synthesis methods so it doesn't all sound the same:

  • FSK — mark/space tones (overlapping the data stream but warped)
  • QAM — dual tones summing (richer, offset)
  • PSK — phase reversals (buzzy, clicky)
  • Chirp — frequency sweeps (sci-fi warble)

And the envelope isn't a smooth fade — it's play-hard, exponential-decay, slam-back-in. Segments of full blast alternating with decaying tails. That stuttering on-off-on-off is the authentic training sound, not a polite fade.

Layer 4: Noise Texture & Bells

Pink noise (proper Voss-McCartney-style, not white) through two resonant state-variable band-pass filters with slowly LFO-modulated cutoffs and amplitude swells. It's the airy "air" on top — the texture that fills the highs without being harsh. Sparse when idle, denser when active.

Bells are plucked tones on send (C5, 523 Hz) and receive (E5, 659 Hz) — sine plus a fifth-partial, exponential decay. The only "event" sounds, kept soft and short. A nod to the one-shot sounds we started with, now as accents in a living bed rather than the whole experience.

The Master Bus: Being Kind to Ears

Here's a detail born directly from that first feedback — "it sounds like it's about to hit." Some people (me included) are sensitive to specific harsh frequency ranges — the kind of thing that makes you flinch. So the master bus has a misophonia-safe EQ: gentle biquad notches at 2.5, 3.5, and 4.5 kHz, the frequencies that cut. Minus 4–5 dB. It takes the edge off without dulling the sound.

Then a soft limiter (x / (1 + |x|*0.5)) to catch peaks gracefully, and a master volume. No harsh clipping, ever. The whole engine is designed to be pleasant to live with — you're going to hear it a lot.

Everything Is a Knob

The final touch: every layer has independent controls, surfaced in Settings. Volume per layer (drone, noise, FSK, training, bells). Tempo (60–160 BPM). Delay multiplier and feedback. Drone filter darkness. Chord octave range. Sparseness. The engine isn't a fixed soundscape — it's an instrument you can tune to taste. Want it sparse and dark? Turn down the noise, darken the drone, lengthen the delay. Want it busy and bright? Crank everything.

So, Why?

You can build a chat app without any of this. A boop and a bloop would do. But here's what I learned: sound is the fastest path to identity.

The moment the app stopped playing notifications and started breathing — a drone that intensifies when the agent is generating, modem chatter that carries its actual words, a reverb tail that lingers after it's done — it stopped being "an app with an AI in it" and became a thing. A presence. Something you want to open not just to get a task done, but because it feels good to be in.

600 lines of Kotlin. Zero audio files. A 1970s modem standard. Some dub techno. And a coding companion that finally sounded like itself.
